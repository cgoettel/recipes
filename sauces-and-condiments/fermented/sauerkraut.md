# Sauerkraut

A bright, crisp, lightly-fermented sauerkraut in the Claussen school — built to be the kraut you reach for both fresh (on a Reuben, on a dog) and in cooking (brat braises, choucroute). Targets shorter ferment, higher salt, thicker cuts, and cold storage to land in fresh-and-crunchy territory rather than deeply funky. Yields about 1 quart.

## Ingredients

- 1 medium head fresh green cabbage (~2 lb / ~900 g after coring), shredded into ~¼" ribbons
- ~20–23 g kosher salt (2.25–2.5% of cabbage weight after shredding — weigh both)
- 1 T juniper berries, lightly crushed (optional but classic)
- 1 T whole brown or yellow mustard seeds (optional)

## Instructions

1. Pull and reserve one or two clean outer leaves of the cabbage — you'll use them as a barrier on top of the shredded cabbage in the jar. Core the head and slice it into ~¼" ribbons. A sharp knife works for one head; a mandoline or food processor with a slicing disc is the move for more.
1. Weigh the shredded cabbage in a large non-reactive bowl. Calculate 2.25–2.5% of that weight in salt and add it (e.g., 22 g salt for 950 g cabbage). Add the juniper and mustard seeds if using.
1. Massage the salt into the cabbage. Squeeze, crush, and work the leaves with your hands for 5–10 minutes until the cabbage goes from crunchy and dry to wet, limp, and producing a pool of brine in the bowl. This is the workout — your hands will be tired.
1. Pack the cabbage tightly into a wide-mouth quart mason jar, pressing down hard with a fist or a tamper to release more brine and eliminate air pockets. Leave 1½" of headspace.
1. Pour any brine left in the bowl over the cabbage. The brine should cover the packed cabbage; if it doesn't, press the cabbage again and wait a few minutes for more brine to come up. Only top off with extra brine (1 t salt per 1 C filtered water) as a last resort.
1. Fold a reserved cabbage leaf and tuck it on top to keep loose shreds submerged. Place a fermentation weight on the leaf. Cap with an airlock fermentation lid.
1. Ferment at room temperature for **7–10 days**. At a typical climate-controlled room (~22°C), expect a faster, funkier-leaning ferment than the textbook 3-week timeline — taste daily starting day 5 with a clean spoon and pull as soon as it tastes pleasantly sour and crisp, before it tips into deeper funk. Days 1–3 will be very active (bubbling, possibly some foam). Activity slows by day 5. Clean lactic acidity arrives anywhere from day 7 to day 10.
1. Transfer to the refrigerator. Cold storage essentially halts further fermentation; the kraut stays bright and crisp for months. Flavor continues to integrate slowly in the cold but won't push further into funky territory.

## Notes

**Why thicker cuts and higher salt.** Standard German technique pushes for paper-thin slicing and ~2% salt — choices that produce a softer, more deeply-fermented kraut. Targeting Claussen-style instead means going the other direction: thicker cuts for residual crunch, higher salt for firmer cell walls and a slower ferment. Without these adjustments, a warm room (anything above ~18°C / 65°F) will push the kraut past the bright/crisp window quickly and into deeper-funky territory.

**Temperature reality.** The textbook ideal for sauerkraut fermentation is 60–65°F (15.5–18.5°C). A typical climate-controlled home runs warmer — usually 70–75°F. That's not a deal-breaker, but it compresses the timeline: what might take 3 weeks at 60°F can be done in 8–10 days at 22°C, with a narrower window for catching the right moment. **Taste daily from day 5 onward** — the difference between "perfect" and "too funky" can be 48 hours at this temperature.

**Chlorinated water kills the ferment.** Municipal tap water has chlorine that inhibits Lactobacillus. Use filtered, bottled, or boiled-and-cooled water if you need to top off the brine. Distilled is fine too.

**Kahm yeast vs mold.** A thin white film on the brine surface is kahm yeast — harmless but tastes off; skim it off if it appears. Actual mold (fuzzy, colored — green, blue, black, pink) means start over. The cabbage leaf barrier on top reduces both risks meaningfully.

**Aromatics.** Skip caraway entirely. The juniper berries and mustard seeds above are the Bavarian/loop-closing pair, but other clean options work: 1 t lightly-cracked black peppercorns, 1 bay leaf, 2–3 thinly-sliced garlic cloves, 1 t fennel seeds. Add at the start with the salt, not at the end. Avoid apple (sweet), cinnamon/allspice/cloves (warming spices), and kimchi/gochugaru directions.

**Variations.**

- **Red cabbage kraut** — same recipe, swap red for green. Deep magenta brine, slightly more tannic flavor, gorgeous color.
- **Mixed green + red** — visually striking when you pull it out of the jar.
- **Carrot kraut** — shredded carrot with 20–30% cabbage for structure, plus 1–2 T grated fresh ginger. Sweeter and snappier; a vegetable side rather than a condiment.
- **Beet kraut** — shredded beet + cabbage; earthy, deep red, holds up to rich foods.
- **Curtido** (Salvadoran) — shredded cabbage + carrot + onion + 1 T dried oregano + a splash of vinegar at the end; bright, lighter, traditionally served with pupusas.

**Use cases.** On a brat with mustard, the canonical pairing. Co-starring in a beer/onion/mustard braise for brats — sauté sliced onion in olive oil until aromatic but not caramelized, add a generous spoonful of whole grain mustard plus half a jar of this kraut, deglaze with about a quarter can of amber ale (Fat Tire is excellent here), simmer while brats grill, serve over the top. On a Reuben or a grilled cheese. On a hot dog. Stirred into mashed potatoes. As a sharp side with rich braised meats. Forked straight from the jar.
