# Salsa — Aji Amarillo (Fermented)

A lacto-fermented hot sauce built around Peruvian aji amarillo chiles. Fruity, floral, almost passion-fruit aromatic — distinctly different from anything in the supermarket hot sauce aisle. Pairs naturally with grilled chicken, ceviche, roasted potatoes, and anywhere a Peruvian dish wants a finishing kick. Yields about 24 oz. Active time ~30 minutes; fermentation runs 2–4 weeks.

The fermentation process is identical to [Salsa — Fresno (Fermented)](sauce-fresno-fermented.md). See that recipe's Notes for equipment, salt math, the chlorinated-water warning, and kahm-yeast-vs-mold guidance.

## Ingredients

- 1 lb (~450 g) fresh or frozen-thawed aji amarillo chiles, stems removed (see Notes on sourcing)
- 4 oz (~115 g) yellow bell pepper, seeds and ribs removed (optional, reinforces yellow color and adds body)
- 1 small white onion (~3 oz / ~85 g), peeled
- 6 cloves garlic, peeled
- ~25 g kosher salt (~2.5% of total vegetable weight)
- Filtered (non-chlorinated) water, as needed
- Optional finish: 2 T fresh lime juice and 1 T olive oil, stirred in after blending

## Instructions

1. Roughly chop the chiles (seeds in for full heat; scrape out for milder), bell pepper, onion, and garlic into ~½" pieces.
1. Weigh everything in a non-reactive bowl. Add salt at 2.5% of the total weight (about 25 g for the quantities above). Mix and let sit 15 minutes so liquid draws out.
1. Pack firmly into a quart wide-mouth mason jar, leaving 1½" headspace.
1. Add filtered water to cover by ½", keeping 1" headspace.
1. Weigh the solids down and cap with an airlock fermentation lid.
1. Ferment at room temperature (65–75°F) for 2–4 weeks. Activity follows the same pattern as the Fresno ferment: very active days 1–5, slowing through week 1–2, deepening through week 4.
1. Strain solids; reserve brine. Blend the solids, adding brine until the consistency is right.
1. Stir in lime juice and olive oil if using. Bottle into clean glass and refrigerate.

## Notes

**Sourcing.** Fresh aji amarillo are hard to find in the US outside select Latin/Hispanic grocers — call ahead. **Frozen** aji amarillo (Goya is the common brand) is the most reliable source; check the freezer section of any well-stocked Latin grocery. Thaw fully and drain off the thaw liquid before using.

**What not to use:** aji amarillo paste in jars (Goya, Inca's Food, etc.) is already cooked and processed. It will not ferment — it will sit and eventually spoil. This recipe needs raw chiles only, fresh or frozen-thawed.

**Heat level.** Aji amarillo runs about 30,000–50,000 SHU — roughly cayenne territory, meaningfully hotter than jalapeño or Fresno but well below habanero. The heat is bright and quick, not lingering.

**The lime + olive oil finish.** A nod to traditional Peruvian aji amarillo sauce (which uses the cooked paste plus oil and lime). The lime adds brightness the ferment alone doesn't; the oil rounds the texture. Both are optional — skip for a leaner, strictly-fermented profile.

**Use cases.** Pollo a la brasa, ceviche, grilled fish, finishing on roasted potatoes (especially Andean varieties), stirred into mayo for sandwiches and fries, brushed on chicken before grilling.

**Variations.**

- **Rocoto instead of aji amarillo.** Rocoto is a related Andean chile — hotter (50,000–100,000 SHU), more vegetal, with distinctive black seeds. Same fermentation approach; even harder to source than aji amarillo. Worth it if you find them.
- **Add huacatay** (Peruvian black mint) if you can source it: 1 T fresh leaves at the start. Adds the herbal-anise note characteristic of Peruvian green sauces.
- **Half aji amarillo + half aji limo.** Aji limo is smaller, slightly hotter, with a citrus-forward profile. The combination is more aromatic.
