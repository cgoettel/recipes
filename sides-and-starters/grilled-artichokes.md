# Grilled Artichokes

A reverse-engineering of the grilled artichoke at Houston's / Hillstone — the benchmark version. Three techniques stacked: **steam** the artichokes tender, **char** them on the grill while mopping repeatedly with garlic-lemon butter, and serve with a cold **remoulade** for dipping. This is written as a method to tune, not a fixed recipe — the ranges are starting points.

## Why it works

- **Steam, don't boil.** Boiling waterlogs the leaves and leaches flavor into the pot. Steaming cooks them tender while they stay dense enough to take a hard char later.
- **Butter, not oil, for the mop — and mop in layers.** Oil only lubricates; butter's milk solids brown against the char and its fat carries the garlic and lemon *into* the leaf. Basting repeatedly as you turn builds successive browned coats. This is the part that reads as "better than everywhere else." Houston's line cooks reportedly use straight salt, pepper, and butter — no olive oil.
- **Real char.** The leaf tips should go genuinely dark, not just marked. Bitterness from the char is what the rich butter and tangy remoulade play against.
- **Smoke is the hidden ingredient.** Houston's grills over hardwood. If you have charcoal or a wood chunk, use it — butter plus wood smoke is the flavor that gas-with-the-lid-up can't reach. On gas, lean harder on the mop and push the char further to compensate.

## Ingredients

**Artichokes**

- 2 large globe artichokes (feel heavy for their size, tight leaves, squeak when squeezed)
- 1 lemon, halved (for the steaming water and for rubbing cut faces)

**Garlic-lemon butter mop**

- ½ cup (1 stick) butter
- 4 cloves garlic, smashed
- 1 strip lemon zest + juice of ½ lemon
- ¾ tsp salt
- Several grinds black pepper

**Remoulade**

- ¾ cup mayonnaise
- 1 Tbsp Creole or whole-grain mustard
- 1 tsp Dijon
- Juice of ½ lemon
- 1 clove garlic, minced or grated
- 1 Tbsp minced cornichon or capers
- 1 green onion, finely sliced
- 1 Tbsp chopped parsley
- Pinch cayenne
- Salt to taste

## Method

### Remoulade (make first, it wants to rest)

1. Stir everything together. Taste and adjust — it should be tangy and a little sharp to cut the butter. Refrigerate at least an hour so the garlic and cayenne bloom.

### The mop

1. Melt the butter gently. Off the heat, add the smashed garlic and lemon zest and let it steep 10 minutes so the butter takes on their flavor without frying the garlic.
2. Stir in the lemon juice, salt, and pepper. Keep it warm and pourable while you grill — the garlic pieces staying in the mop is fine.

### Prep and steam

1. Cut the top inch off each artichoke. Trim the sharp leaf tips with scissors. Halve lengthwise and scoop out the fuzzy choke and any sharp inner purple leaves with a spoon.
2. Rub every cut and exposed surface with a lemon half to stop browning.
3. Steam cut-side down over ~1 inch of simmering water, covered, 20–25 minutes, until a knife slips easily into the heart. (Pressure cooker: ~8 minutes. The goal is fully tender — undercooked here means tough leaves off the grill.)
4. Pat the halves **very** dry, especially the cut face. Surface water steams instead of chars and dilutes the butter.

### Grill

1. Heat the grill hot (~400°F+). Charcoal or a wood chunk if you have it.
2. Brush the cut face generously with the mop. Lay cut-side down and grill 4–5 minutes for a hard, dark char.
3. Flip, mop the cut face again, and grill 4–5 minutes on the back.
4. Flip cut-side down once more, mop a third time, 1–2 minutes to set the last coat. **Three mop passes minimum** — that layering is the point.
5. Serve hot with the remoulade. Eat by pulling leaves and dragging the meaty base through the sauce; finish with the heart.

## Tuning notes

Things to push around across attempts:

- **Char depth** — the single biggest lever. Go darker than feels comfortable the first time and dial back if it turns acrid.
- **Mop frequency** — try mopping every time you touch the grate, even between the passes above. More layers, more depth.
- **Butter treatment** — brown the butter before steeping the garlic for a nuttier mop, or add a splash of the steaming lemon water to emulsify it slightly so it clings.
- **Smoke** — a single hardwood chunk (oak, a fruitwood) is likely the biggest gap between a home version and the restaurant's. Add it and judge before changing anything else.
- **Remoulade sharpness** — if the dish reads too rich, take the sauce more acidic and mustard-forward.
