# Kyohana-style curry rice

Rebuilt from a meal at Kyohana, a restaurant in Kyoto, and from watching how it was made. No boxed roux bricks and no meat in the sauce: it's a smooth, glossy curry served over rice with a tonkatsu laid alongside. Its body comes from a deeply browned giblet stock, rich enough in collagen that leftovers set to a firm jelly in the fridge. If yours does that, you got it right.

Because there's nothing in the sauce to hide behind, the method has three stages, and each one adds depth: browning the giblets for fond, simmering a gelatin-heavy stock, and building a fresh roux with spices you toast yourself. Start the stock a day ahead. Curry is better on day two anyway.

## Ingredients

### Giblet stock (makes about 2 L)

- 1–1½ lb chicken giblets and necks (gizzards, hearts, necks; leave out the liver, which turns bitter and cloudy)
- 1–1½ lb chicken feet, wings, or backs (feet are the collagen powerhouse; use at least some)
- 1 tablespoon neutral oil
- 1 onion, halved, skin on
- 2" piece ginger, sliced
- 4–6 garlic cloves, smashed
- Green tops of a bunch of green onions
- About 3 L cold water

### Spice blend

- 2 tablespoons coriander seed
- 1 tablespoon cumin seed
- 1 teaspoon fennel seed
- ½ teaspoon fenugreek seed
- Seeds from 4–5 green cardamom pods
- 1 teaspoon black peppercorns
- 1–2 dried red chilies, or ½–1 teaspoon cayenne
- 1 tablespoon ground turmeric
- ¼ teaspoon freshly grated nutmeg
- 1 bay leaf
- Optional: 1 teaspoon dried orange peel (chenpi), ground

### Curry

- 2–3 large yellow onions, thinly sliced
- 2 tablespoons chicken fat skimmed from the stock, or neutral oil
- 1 carrot, grated (melts into the sauce for body)
- 3–4 garlic cloves, grated
- 1" piece ginger, grated
- 1 tablespoon tomato paste
- ¾ stick (6 tablespoons) butter
- ½ cup flour
- 1–1½ L giblet stock
- 1–2 tablespoons soy sauce
- 1 tablespoon Worcestershire sauce
- Salt to taste

### To serve

- Steamed short-grain rice
- Pork tonkatsu, sliced

## Instructions

### Stock (the day before)

1. Pat the giblets and necks dry. Heat the oil in a heavy stockpot over medium-high heat and brown them in batches without crowding. Leave them alone until they release from the pot. You want a dark brown fond across the bottom, not a pale one. This is where most of the curry's depth comes from.
2. Put the halved onion cut-side down in the same pot and char it hard.
3. Splash in a cup of water and scrape up every bit of fond.
4. Add the feet, wings, or backs, the ginger, garlic, green-onion tops, and enough cold water to cover by an inch or two.
5. Bring slowly to a bare simmer and skim the gray foam that rises in the first 20 minutes. Keep it at a lazy bubble, never a boil. Boiling emulsifies the fat and clouds the stock, and gentle heat pulls out more gelatin. Simmer 4–6 hours, topping up with water if the bones poke through.
6. Strain, cool, and refrigerate overnight. It should set firm. If it's loose, reduce it by a third and test again. Lift off the fat cap and save a couple of spoonfuls for the onions.

### Spice blend

1. Toast the whole seeds, peppercorns, chilies, and bay leaf in a dry skillet over medium heat, shaking the pan, until fragrant and a shade darker (2–3 minutes). Don't let the fenugreek go past light brown, or it turns bitter.
2. Cool, then grind fine and stir in the turmeric, nutmeg, and orange peel if using. Nutmeg and orange peel are both standard in Japanese curry blends: nutmeg adds warmth without sweetness, and the peel adds a bitter-citrus aroma that brings the blend closer to the shop flavor.

### Curry

1. Caramelize the onions: cook them in the chicken fat over medium to medium-low heat for 45–60 minutes, stirring more often as they darken, until deep mahogany and jammy. Add the grated carrot for the last 15 minutes. Deglaze with a splash of stock whenever the bottom starts to catch. Don't rush this. These onions supply the sweetness that bricks get from sugar.
2. Add the garlic, ginger, and tomato paste. Cook 2–3 minutes, until the paste darkens.
3. Make the roux in a separate small pan: melt the butter, whisk in the flour, and cook over medium-low heat, stirring constantly, until it smells nutty and turns peanut-butter brown (10–15 minutes). Take it off the heat and stir in 3–4 tablespoons of the spice blend. The heat of the roux blooms the spices without scorching them.
4. Add 1 L of the stock to the onion pot and bring to a simmer.
5. Blend completely smooth with an immersion blender (or in batches in a countertop blender). Doing this before the roux goes in gets the onions finer than blending a thickened sauce.
6. Whisk in the roux a ladle at a time until smooth.
7. Simmer gently, uncovered, 20–30 minutes, stirring the bottom often since roux-thickened sauces scorch. Thin with more stock as it tightens. It should coat a spoon thickly and look glossy.
8. Season with soy sauce and Worcestershire, then salt. Taste for spice and add more of the blend if it needs it. Simmer 5 more minutes after any addition. For a perfectly silky shop finish, pass it through a fine strainer.
9. Serve over rice with sliced tonkatsu alongside, or cool and refrigerate overnight. It will set to jelly and taste better reheated.

## Notes

- **The jelly test.** Collagen from the feet and wings is what makes the curry set when cold. If it doesn't set, use more feet next time or reduce the stock further. Reheat gently and loosen with a splash of water.
- **Shortcut spices.** S&B curry powder (the red can) can stand in for the homemade blend. It's still far better than bricks, and it includes cinnamon and clove. To freshen it, toast and grind 1 tablespoon coriander seed and 1–2 teaspoons cumin seed and stir them in with the S&B.
