# Mustard — Whole Grain

A Maille-style whole grain. Plump intact seeds bound by a wine-vinegar paste, mellowed by a couple weeks in the fridge. Fresh out of the processor it tastes bitter and acrid — that is correct, do not try to fix it. The transformation happens during the rest. Yields about 1½ cups (one jar).

## Ingredients

- ½ C brown mustard seeds
- ⅓ C yellow mustard seeds
- ½ C dry white wine (Sauvignon Blanc, dry Riesling, Pinot Grigio, or dry unoaked Chardonnay)
- ⅓ C white wine vinegar
- ⅓ C water
- 1 t kosher salt
- 2 t honey

## Instructions

1. In a non-reactive bowl or jar, combine the brown and yellow seeds with the wine, vinegar, and water. Stir, cover, and refrigerate at least 24 hours, up to 48. The seeds will absorb most of the liquid and roughly double in size.
1. Transfer the soaked seeds and any remaining liquid to a food processor. Pulse 5–15 times in short bursts. Aim for about a third of the seeds broken open and the rest intact, bound by a coarse paste. Stop early — you can always pulse more, but you cannot un-crush seeds.
1. Stir in the salt and honey. Taste it; expect it to be bitter and harsh. The mellowing happens during aging, not now.
1. Transfer to a clean glass jar and refrigerate at least 1 week before tasting again, ideally 2–3 weeks. Keeps for months in the fridge.

## Notes

Mustard is acidic and picks up off-flavors from metal — use glass jars and plastic or wood utensils throughout.

### Variables worth playing with

- **Seed ratio.** Base is ~3:2 brown to yellow. More brown (2:1, 3:1, or all brown) gives a hotter, more assertive mustard. More yellow (1:1, 1:2) gives something milder and tangier. Maille looks closer to 1:1 by eye.
- **Liquid composition.** Base is roughly 3:2:2 wine:vinegar:water. More wine softens and adds vinous character (closer to a Burgundy bistro style). More vinegar sharpens the acid bite. Total volume matters less than coverage — keep seeds well-covered at the start.
- **Sweetener.** 2 t honey is barely-there roundness. Skip entirely for something more aggressive; push to 1 T for noticeably honey-mustard. Brown sugar swaps in for a more molasses-leaning profile.
- **Add-ins.** Stir in before aging so they integrate: 1 t cracked black peppercorns, a few crushed garlic cloves, fresh tarragon, 1 T grated fresh horseradish (big upgrade), juniper berries, a pinch of allspice.
- **Texture.** For more whole seeds and less paste (Maille-like), soak the full 48 hours — softer seeds break less aggressively — and pulse fewer times. For more spreadable, pulse more, or pull out half the mixture before pulsing and stir it back in after.

### Beer swap (1:1 for wine)

Beer is ~5% ABV vs wine's ~12%, but close enough to substitute directly. The catch is hops: IPAs and aggressively hopped beers get more bitter with aging, sometimes unpleasantly. Hop-balanced ales work great — Sierra Nevada Pale Ale, English bitter like Fuller's London Pride, Kona Longboard. Stouts and porters give a roasty, almost coffee-adjacent direction — pair with brown sugar instead of honey. Carbonation doesn't matter; it dissipates during the soak. Beer has essentially no acid, so bump the vinegar share slightly: try ~2:1.5:1 beer:vinegar:water.

### Spirit accents

Spirits at 40%+ ABV are too aggressive to substitute 1:1 — harsh ethanol notes don't mellow because the mustard never gets heated. Two ways to handle:

- **Accent:** 1–3 T of spirit in place of an equal amount of wine.
- **Dilution:** 1 part spirit to 3–4 parts water as the "wine" replacement.

Directions worth trying:

- **Bourbon** with maple in place of honey — fall sandwich staple.
- **Rye** — spicier.
- **Calvados / apple brandy** — pork pairing, fall/winter.
- **Mezcal** — smoky, one-trick pony but great with grilled.
- **Gin** (juniper-forward like Tanqueray or Beefeater, not a soft contemporary style) — 2–3 T plus 1 t whole juniper berries and a strip of lemon peel in the soak. The botanical profile is already mustard-adjacent.

### Amari (finishing accent only)

Stir 1–2 T in after pulsing, before aging. Easy to overdo — too much makes a confused candy.

- **Cynar** — artichoke-vegetal bitterness, gorgeous on roast pork or sandwiches. Standout candidate.
- **Averna** — caramel and citrus, dessert-leaning.
- **Fernet** — polarizing but interesting in small doses.
- **Nonino** — orange and herbal, softer entry point.

### Other fortified / aromatic swaps

- **Dry vermouth** (Dolin, Noilly Prat) substitutes for wine perfectly — it's herbal-fortified wine.
- **Dry sherry** (fino or amontillado) gives nutty oxidative depth.
- **Madeira** pairs with a honey-mustard direction.

### Starting experiments

Two worth running back-to-back: straight **English bitter** (1:1 swap for the wine) and a **bourbon** version (3 T bourbon, drop water to 2 T, swap honey for maple). They'll teach the most about how the flavor base interacts with aging.
